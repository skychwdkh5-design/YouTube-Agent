## The first fifteen seconds
1. Saudi Arabia announced a city 170 kilometers long. That is about 105 miles.
2. So we pointed free satellite images at the same stretch of desert, year after year.
3. In 2022, a faint line.
4. By 2026, a long straight corridor, and one dark segment between 1.6 and 1.9 kilometers long.
5. Here is what that is, and what it is not.

## The promise
6. The project is called The Line, part of NEOM, on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast.
7. It was announced as one straight city, 170 kilometers long.
8. The frame on screen is only a fraction of that length. The full line would run far past both edges.
9. That promise is the first of three things we will keep apart in this video.
10. The second is what the news has reported. The third is what a satellite can actually see.
11. We will not mix them.

## What has been reported
12. First, the plans. In April 2024, Bloomberg reported that only about 2.4 kilometers of The Line was expected by 2030.
13. It also reported fewer than 300,000 residents by then.
14. In May 2026, Semafor reported that NEOM had halted work on The Line until at least after 2030, citing unnamed sources.
15. It has not been officially confirmed.
16. In October 2026, Reuters reported that the NEOM stadium was shelved indefinitely, with no official announcement.
17. These are news reports, not official statements.
18. We could not open the original articles from our research setup, so we rely on how other outlets described them.
19. Each report was described by at least two other outlets. We say who reported what, and nothing more.

## How we looked
20. So what can a satellite add? Not opinions. Just pixels.
21. We used free Sentinel-2 images from the European Copernicus program.
22. Each pixel in our frames covers 10 meters of ground.
23. That is sharp enough to see a long cleared strip or a road, but not a person, a crane, or a window.
24. We picked seven scenes from the same orbit, taken in late September or October of each year from 2020 to 2026.
25. Same orbit means the satellite passes over on the same track at nearly the same time of day, so the views can be compared from year to year.
26. Same season means the light and the sand look more alike, so a change is less likely to be a trick of the light.
27. The scenes report almost no cloud cover, and we looked at every frame.

## What the satellites show
28. Start with 2020 and 2021, when the ground here is empty desert.
29. In October 2022, a faint, straight line appears.
30. By 2023, the line is a clear, narrow corridor, and very straight.
31. It crosses our frame for at least 15 kilometers.
32. And it is still there in 2026.
33. Along the way, two large settlement-like complexes, one at each end of our frame, grow between 2020 and 2024.
34. From orbit, we cannot tell what they are.
35. What we can say is that something large changed on this ground, in a straight line.
36. What we cannot say is what the corridor is for.

## The dark segment
37. Now zoom in on one stretch of that corridor.
38. In October 2022 and 2023, nothing dark is here.
39. In October 2024, a dark segment appears.
40. In 2025 and 2026, it is still there, about 1.6 to 1.9 kilometers long.
41. In near-infrared light, the segment reads like water: our water index is above zero, and the near-infrared signal is very low.
42. That fits water, but it is not proof.
43. A shadow can also fool a water index, and we cannot rule that out from these images alone.
44. In 2022 and 2023, no pixel here qualifies.
45. That is consistent with one idea: standing water in a long, narrow excavation.
46. But that is a hypothesis, and it could be wrong.

## How we tried to break it
47. We tried to break our own result.
48. First, was it a one-day accident? No. The segment appears on three different dates and on three different Sentinel-2 satellites in 2024, 2025 and 2026.
49. Second, does a different satellite agree? Landsat 8, at 30 meters per pixel, shows the same segment, but measures it differently.
50. It gives about 0.7 kilometers in October 2024, where Sentinel-2 says 1.6.
51. It gives about 1.65 kilometers in October 2025 and September 2026.
52. The two sets of scenes are two to three weeks apart, so we cannot tell whether the gap is resolution or timing.
53. Third, how long is it? At 10 meters, about 1.6 kilometers in October 2025, and about 1.9 kilometers in October 2026.
54. That difference of 0.3 kilometers could be growth. It could also be a different water level, or just where our threshold falls.
55. So we do not claim that the segment grew. We cannot tell.
56. That is the honest result: three checks agree it is real, and one number refuses to settle.

## What we cannot say
57. So what can we not say?
58. At this resolution, we cannot identify any building along the corridor itself.
59. We also cannot say this is a finished city.
60. We cannot prove that this corridor is The Line.
61. We had no official NEOM map to match it against, and matching would take sources we could not open.
62. A similar picture in 2025 and 2026 does not prove a pause.
63. It is only compatible with what Semafor reported.
64. Official figures and the full original reports would change how sure we can be.
