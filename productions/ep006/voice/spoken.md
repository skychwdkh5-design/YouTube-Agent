For seven weeks before 26 August 2026, a radar satellite had been picking up movement in a mountain slope in northern Nepal.

What had the satellites seen? And what does it not prove?

On 12 August 2026, this valley looks quiet.

Fourteen days later, a dark swath runs through the same valley.

First, what officials and news outlets said.

The US Geological Survey said a rapid slope failure involving a glacier struck Langtang National Park in Nepal, near the Chinese border, on the morning of 26 August 2026.

It said the debris flow and flood traveled about 100 kilometers, roughly 62 miles, along two rivers, the Lende Khola and the Trishuli Khola.

It said the flood hit populated areas downstream, including the Rasuwagadhi border post.

Its summary does not say where along that route the water stopped.

The failure itself shook the ground. USGS said it released seismic energy equal to a magnitude 5.2 earthquake.

USGS said a second signal, about three hours later, was equal to magnitude 4.2.

USGS said those are energy comparisons: the slope failure itself produced the signals.

USGS also said it has not yet determined whether this began as a landslide that picked up part of a glacier, or as a failure of the glacier itself.

And it said everything it has published is preliminary.

This mountain has done something like this before. In 2015, USGS said, a magnitude 7.8 earthquake set off a debris avalanche on the same mountain.

USGS said it fell nearly 1,900 meters, buried a village, and killed more than 200 people.

Now the human cost. By mid-October, Nepali figures reported more than 1,400 people dead in Nepal, and roughly five thousand still listed as missing.

Those lists keep changing as names are checked, so we are not giving one exact number.

China counts its own side of the border separately. Xinhua reported 43 dead and 519 missing at Gyirong Port as of 5 September.

It was not clear whether some people appear in both countries' counts, so we do not add the two together.

Satellites cannot see people. They can see ground, ice, cloud, and sometimes tiny movements of a slope.

The mountain research organization ICIMOD published a preliminary analysis on 13 October.

It said the disaster was not a conventional glacial lake outburst flood.

It said a failure in the bedrock sheared away part of the glacier lying on top of it.

ICIMOD also said that radar satellites, Sentinel-1, detected up to 40 centimeters of ground movement between 7 July and 24 August.

That is movement along the satellite's line of sight, not the full motion of the slope.

And the period ended two days before the failure.

We do not claim this was a warning anyone could have acted on. No source we found shows that.

ICIMOD's early estimate of the volume was about 132 million cubic meters of rock and ice.

Another early analysis suggested a much smaller volume.

So the size is not settled, and we do not rely on one number.

Now our own images. They come from free public data.

First we looked for the clearest view before the event.

The best was 12 August, with 19 percent cloud cover over the scene.

But between 14 and 27 August, every Sentinel-2 pass had between 39 and 97 percent cloud cover.

Radar does not have that cloud problem, but the radar numbers in this video are ICIMOD's, not ours.

So for 26 August we used a different satellite, Landsat 9.

Its pixels are 30 meters wide, three times coarser than Sentinel-2, and we show them as blocks, without sharpening.

In the 12 August image, the river is a narrow pale line.

In the 26 August image, a dark grey-brown swath follows the same valley.

From the image alone, we cannot tell what the swath is made of.

We traced it by eye. On our grid it runs about 30 kilometers.

About 20 of them lie between the glacier and the point where the river turns south, near the Rasuwagadhi border post.

Clouds hide other stretches, so this is the least we can follow, not the full length.

USGS said the flow traveled about 100 kilometers.

Our frame cannot show all of it.

USGS said it is mapping the full extent by comparing satellite images from before and after.

Our trace is not a substitute for that official map.

What can we not say?

These images show what the ground looked like on two days. They are not a measurement of the flood itself.

We cannot say how fast the flow moved, or how high the water rose in any one village. These images do not show that.

We cannot say why the slope failed, because that work is not finished.

We cannot say anyone could have been warned in time.

And we cannot say how many people died, because the lists are not final.

Two things will settle more of this.

The final findings from USGS and ICIMOD, on the type and size of the failure, are not out yet.

And the official counts of the dead and missing, on both sides of the border, are not final.
