# EP006 - The Mountain That Fell (working title)

## hook: The first fifteen seconds
On 12 August 2026, this valley in northern Nepal, high in the Himalaya, looks quiet. {C13}

## plan: What has been reported
Fourteen days later, a dark swath runs through the same valley. {C13}
For seven weeks before that, a radar satellite had been picking up movement in a mountain slope in northern Nepal. {C8}
What had the satellites seen? And what does it not prove? {L}
First, what officials and news outlets said.
The US Geological Survey said a rapid slope failure involving a glacier struck Langtang National Park in Nepal, near the Chinese border, on the morning of 26 August 2026. {C1}
It said the debris flow and flood traveled about 100 kilometers, roughly 62 miles, along two rivers, the Lende Khola and the Trishuli Khola. {C2}
It said the flood hit populated areas downstream, including the Rasuwagadhi border post. {C2}
Its summary does not say where along that route the water stopped. {L}
The failure itself shook the ground. USGS said it released seismic energy equal to a magnitude 5.2 earthquake. {C3}
USGS said a second signal, about three hours later, was equal to magnitude 4.2. {C3}
USGS said those are energy comparisons: the slope failure itself produced the signals. {C3}
USGS also said it has not yet determined whether this began as a landslide that picked up part of a glacier, or as a failure of the glacier itself. {C4}
And it said everything it has published is preliminary. {C4}
This mountain has done something like this before. In 2015, USGS said, a magnitude 7.8 earthquake set off a debris avalanche on the same mountain. {C5}
USGS said it fell nearly 1,900 meters, buried a village, and killed more than 200 people. {C5}
Now the human cost. In its 13 October release, ICIMOD reported figures from Nepal's disaster authority: 1,451 people dead and 5,745 missing in Nepal. {C10}
Other lists differ slightly and keep changing, so these numbers are not final. {L}
China counts its own side of the border separately. Xinhua reported 43 dead and 519 missing at Gyirong Port as of 5 September. {C11}
It was not clear whether some people appear in both countries' counts, so we do not add the two together. {L}

## method: What satellites saw first
Satellites cannot see people on the ground. They can see ground, ice, cloud, and sometimes tiny movements of a slope.
The mountain research organization ICIMOD published a preliminary analysis on 13 October. {C7}
It said the disaster was not a conventional glacial lake outburst flood. {C7}
It said a failure in the bedrock sheared away part of the glacier lying on top of it. {C7}
ICIMOD also said that radar satellites, Sentinel-1, detected up to 40 centimeters of ground movement between 7 July and 24 August. {C8}
That is movement along the satellite's line of sight, not the full motion of the slope. {C8}
And the period ended two days before the failure. {C8}
We do not claim this was a warning anyone could have acted on. No source we found shows that. {L}
ICIMOD's early estimate of the volume was about 132 million cubic meters of rock and ice. {C9}
Another early analysis suggested a much smaller volume. {C9}
So the size is not settled, and we do not rely on one number. {L}

## obs: The path on our images
Now our own images. They come from free public data.
First we looked for the clearest view before the event. {C15}
The best was 12 August, with 19 percent cloud cover over the scene. {C15}
But between 14 and 27 August, every Sentinel-2 pass had between 39 and 97 percent cloud cover. {C15}
Radar does not have that cloud problem, but the radar numbers in this video are ICIMOD's, not ours. {L}
So for 26 August we used a different satellite, Landsat 9. {C16}
Its pixels are 30 meters wide, three times coarser than Sentinel-2, and we show them as blocks, without sharpening. {C16}
In the 12 August image, the river is a narrow pale line. {C13}
In the 26 August image, a dark grey-brown swath follows the same valley. {C13}
From the image alone, we cannot tell what the swath is made of. {L}
We traced it by eye. On our grid it runs about 30 kilometers. {C14}
About 20 of them lie between the glacier and the point where the river turns south, near the Rasuwagadhi border post. {C14}
Clouds hide other stretches, so this is the least we can follow, not the full length. {L}
USGS said the flow traveled about 100 kilometers. {C2}
Our frame cannot show all of it. {L}
USGS said it is mapping the full extent by comparing satellite images from before and after. {C6}
Our trace is not a substitute for that official map. {L}

## limits: What we cannot say
What can we not say?
These images show what the ground looked like on two days. They are not a measurement of the flood itself. {L}
We cannot say how fast the flow moved, or how high the water rose in any one village. These images do not show that. {L}
We cannot say why the slope failed, because that work is not finished. {L}
We cannot say anyone could have been warned in time. {L}
And we cannot say how many people died, because the lists are not final. {L}

## close: What to watch
Two things will settle more of this.
The final findings from USGS and ICIMOD, on the type and size of the failure, are not out yet. {L}
And the official counts of the dead and missing, on both sides of the border, are not final. {L}
Every satellite frame here comes from free public data, with the credits on screen. The sources are in the description.
