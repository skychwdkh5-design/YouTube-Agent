November 2000. A satellite looks down at a patch of the Persian Gulf. There is no island here. Just sea.
November 2003. The same place. A palm tree, fully formed. Between these two pictures: three years.
More than two decades after that first image, an astronaut aboard the International Space Station photographs this coast. The palm-shaped islands stand out from orbit.
So how do you build land where there was only sea? And what happened to the plans that came next?
This is Dubai, in the United Arab Emirates, on the shore of the Persian Gulf. A coastline seems like the one thing on a map that nobody gets to choose. Dubai put that idea to the test. To see how, we go back to the year 2000.
Here is that year, seen by an instrument called ASTER, on NASA's Terra satellite. The city hugs the shore. Inland, there is mostly empty desert. Offshore, where the palm will appear, nothing has been built at all.
NASA's record includes twelve images of this area between 2000 and 2011. This is our baseline.
Dubai's ambitions went far beyond one island. NASA describes the city building hundreds of artificial islands along its coast, to expand its beachfront for tourism. Plans from the 1990s for one project, called The World, included about three hundred small islands.
And as recently as the early 2000s, NASA notes, little had been built here beyond shallow Gulf water. That was the blank page.
February 2002. About fifteen months after the empty-sea picture, the first early stage of an archipelago appears. This is Palm Jumeirah, the first of Dubai's palm islands to be built.
By October 2002, sandy fronds are visible, with a circular breakwater around them. That ring is a clue. This is land with its own defense against open water. NASA calls these circular storm barriers.
So where does the land come from? From the sea floor itself. Sand was dredged from the bottom of the Gulf and placed where the island would be, with rock breakwaters to protect it from erosion. The material for the island was already there, underwater.
But loose sand is not ground you can build on. NASA's description of the method includes two details. Ships guided by GPS placed the sand. And a machine called a vibroflot compacted it with stone, solidifying the land.
Compare October 2002 with November 2003. First, sandy fronds inside their ring. Then, a complete palm. Just over a year apart. The pictures can't show us how the work went day by day, only where it stood on each date. But they do show the ring already in place in October 2002, while the fronds are still sand. The buildings and vegetation come later, in the images from 2004 to 2008.
After 2003, the images change in a different way. From 2004 through 2008, ASTER shows buildings and vegetation appearing on the palm. The land was no longer just a shape. It was becoming a place.
Palm Jumeirah was only the first. By September 2006, NASA was describing the palm islands as the largest artificial islands in the world, and every one of them was still under construction.
That 2006 image shows four projects in a row, from south to north: Palm Jebel Ali, Palm Jumeirah, The World, and Palm Deira. Jebel Ali and Jumeirah already look largely complete as landforms. The World is still under construction. Palm Deira is in its earliest stages.
Palm Jumeirah and Palm Jebel Ali were shaped into palm trees. The third project is not a palm. The World is laid out as a map of Earth's continents. The plans called for about three hundred small islands, arranged to draw the planet in the sea.
Then came 2007 and 2008, and the global recession. According to NASA, it delayed these projects. In the 2006 image, you can see how much was still ahead: Palm Deira had barely begun, and The World was still under construction.
Now jump ahead to 2022. NASA's caption for the astronaut's photograph says only a handful of The World's roughly three hundred islands had seen development. That is a statement about 2022, and we will stay inside it. We have no reliable, dated source for what has happened since, so we will not guess.
Two more projects followed the same pattern. Palm Deira, in its earliest stages in 2006, was scaled back and renamed the Deira Islands. And The Universe, another planned archipelago, was put on hold. Delayed, scaled back, on hold: those words describe plans, not the condition of the land.
NASA describes one basic method for Dubai's islands: dredged sand, protected by rock. One design is a palm. The other is a map of Earth. By 2011, vegetation covered most of Palm Jumeirah's fronds, and many buildings stood on its trunk. In 2022, only a handful of The World's islands had seen development. Similar method. Very different outcomes.
What keeps new land from washing away? In NASA's account, rock breakwaters protect the islands from erosion. Around Palm Jumeirah and Palm Jebel Ali, they form a circular storm barrier: stone on the outside, sand on the inside.
Now widen the view. In 2000, the land inland was mostly empty desert. By 2011, NASA reports, roads, buildings and irrigated land covered almost all of this area. The coast was changing, and so was everything behind it.
A note on method. Every image here carries its source, its instrument and its year. Most come from ASTER, an instrument on a satellite. One is a photograph taken by an astronaut with a Nikon camera. They are separate pictures from separate days, not moving footage, and we never overlay one on another. For one image, NASA's text and caption disagree on the month, so we give only the year.
Lay out the whole record. Twelve ASTER images, from November 2000 to 2011. Empty sea. The first early stage. Sandy fronds inside a ring. A finished palm. Years of buildings and vegetation. Each one is a dated fact. Together, they are a timeline you can check.
NASA says both palm-shaped islands stand out in the 2022 astronaut photograph. Both are ringed by circular storm barriers. But according to NASA's caption, Palm Jebel Ali remains mostly undeveloped, though it already looked largely complete as a landform in 2006. A finished shape is not a finished city. So this is not a story of simple success or failure. It is a record of what was built, what was delayed, and what was only planned.
A map of this coast drawn in 2000 would show open water here. By November 2003, that map would be wrong. The method NASA describes is simple to state: take sand from the sea floor, ring it with rock, and a coastline stops being a fixed line. It gave Dubai a palm. It also gave a plan for a map of Earth.
November 2000: open sea. November 2003: a palm, inside its ring. In 2022, an astronaut photographs the palm islands from orbit. And NASA's caption notes that other projects were delayed, scaled back, or put on hold. A coastline is not only where the land ends. Sometimes, it is a decision. And from orbit, you can check the dates.
