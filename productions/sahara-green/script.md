## COLD OPEN
You could turn the Sahara green. It has happened more than 230 times before. [P20] But this time, what you'd change thousands of miles away is the part that's easy to miss.

The Sahara covers about 9 million square kilometers, roughly 3.5 million square miles. That's bigger than the entire lower 48 United States. [S1, S2] Across most of it, less than 100 millimeters of rain falls in a year. That's under 4 inches. [S3]

So could we really cover it in green? Scientists have run the numbers. Engineers have already done it, in patches. And climate models have tested what happens if we go all the way. But first, the Sahara that was already green.

## ACT 1 — THE GREEN SAHARA
Between roughly 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, much of the Sahara was grassland and savanna, dotted with lakes and wetlands. [P2, P11] Not a jungle. Think of the open plains of East Africa today.

In what is now Chad, a lake called Mega-Chad covered at least 400,000 square kilometers. That's more than 150,000 square miles of water, in a place that is now desert. [P10]

At a site called Gobero, in Niger, archaeologists found about 200 graves beside a vanished lake, among the bones of crocodiles and large fish. [P12] In Algeria's Tassili n'Ajjer, more than 15,000 drawings and engravings record animals and people from a wetter time. [P14]

How much wetter? One of the scientists who reconstructed the ancient rainfall, Jessica Tierney, put it simply: the Sahara was "ten times as wet as today." [P8]

And a piece of that world is still here. In northern Chad, the Lakes of Ounianga sit in the middle of the desert, fed by ancient groundwater. [L4] The mud at the bottom of one of them, Lake Yoa, holds a layer-by-layer record of the Sahara drying out. [P18]

## ACT 2 — WHAT FLIPS THE SWITCH
So what turned the Sahara green? Not people. Earth's orbit.

Earth's orbit has a slow wobble, a cycle of roughly 21,000 years. It changes how much summer sunlight reaches the Northern Hemisphere. When northern summers were sunnier, the West African monsoon pushed its rains far north, deep into the Sahara. [P3]

But sunlight alone can't explain how wet it got. Climate models only match the evidence when they add two amplifiers: plants, and cleaner air with less dust. [P5] More plants meant more rain. More rain meant more plants.

And it's not a one-time event. Sediment records suggest the Sahara has turned green more than 230 times in the last 8 million years. [P20]

How did the last one end? Scientists argued about that for years. A dust record off the coast of Mauritania pointed to a sudden end. [P17] Lake Yoa pointed to a slow one. [P18] The leading explanation today is that both are partly right. At any one place, the end came fairly fast. But it came later the farther south you go. The desert crept south. [P19]

## ACT 3 — WHY IT'S A DESERT NOW
Today, the Sahara sits under a belt of sinking air. Near the equator, warm air rises and drops its rain. High up, it flows toward the poles, then sinks over the subtropics, warming and drying as it falls. [N1 — SOURCE PENDING] That sinking air is a big reason rain clouds struggle to form here.

So greening the Sahara isn't just about finding water. You'd be planting against the atmosphere itself.

## ACT 4 — WE'RE ALREADY DOING IT, IN PATCHES
And yet, from orbit, you can find green in the Sahara right now.

This is East Oweinat, in southern Egypt. In the earliest Landsat images, it's bare desert. Today, it's covered in green circles, each roughly a kilometer across, watered by rotating sprinkler arms. [L1 — SCENE-CHECK]

The water comes from below. The Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System holds more than 150,000 cubic kilometers of mostly ancient groundwater, under Egypt, Libya, Sudan and Chad. [G17] Today, it's barely being refilled. [G18] Every one of these circles runs on water that soaked into the ground during wetter times, thousands of years ago or more. [L2]

Across the border in Libya, the Kufra oasis runs on the same kind of water. [L2] In the 1980s, Libya began building the Great Man-Made River to pipe it north to the coast. [G19 — SOURCE UPGRADE PENDING]

And at Toshka, Egypt let water from Lake Nasser spill into the desert. New lakes appeared in the late 1990s. NASA measured them at up to about 1,450 square kilometers, around 560 square miles. By 2012 they had shrunk by roughly 80 percent. By 2021, they were full again. [L3 — SCENE-CHECK]

So yes, we can green the desert. One field at a time, with water that doesn't come back.

## ACT 5 — THE BIG PLAN
In 2009, three scientists proposed going much bigger: forests of fast-growing trees across the Sahara and the Australian Outback, watered with desalinated seawater through drip irrigation. [G1] They argued these forests could absorb as much carbon dioxide each year as fossil fuels emit. [G2] The estimated price: on the order of two trillion dollars a year. [G4]

Would it rain? In 2018, another team tested an irrigated Sahara forest in a different climate model. The Sahara cooled by about 6 degrees Celsius, around 11 degrees Fahrenheit, and rainfall rose by about 267 millimeters a year, roughly 10 inches. [G5] But only about a quarter of the water the trees gave off fell back as rain over the Sahara. Much of the rest drifted south, and fed the monsoon over the Sahel. [G6] In that model, the forest wouldn't water itself. You'd have to keep pumping.

A different simulation covered the Sahara with wind and solar farms instead. Rainfall more than doubled, and the Sahel gained 200 to 500 millimeters a year. [G8]

## ACT 6 — WHAT YOU'D CHANGE FAR AWAY
Now, the part that's easy to miss.

First, color. Sand reflects a lot of sunlight. Trees and solar panels are darker, so they absorb more of it. A 2022 study of forests planted in drylands found that this extra warming cancels out much of their carbon benefit. [G20] And a 2021 model found that solar panels over a fifth of the Sahara would warm the whole planet by about 0.16 degrees Celsius, and shift rain away from places like the Amazon. [G10, G11]

Second, dust. NASA satellite data suggest that, on average, about 182 million tons of Saharan dust blow out over the Atlantic every year, and roughly 28 million tons settle on the Amazon basin. [S4, S5] That dust carries an estimated 22,000 tons of phosphorus a year, about as much as the rainforest loses to rain and floods. [S6]

One of the biggest sources is here: the Bodélé Depression in Chad, the dry floor of old Lake Mega-Chad, covered in the fossil skeletons of tiny algae. [S7, S9] Green the Sahara, and much of that dust could disappear. What that would mean for the Amazon is an open question. No study has measured it for a planted Sahara. [G14]

Third, storms. In a climate model of the last Green Sahara, adding plants and removing dust increased tropical cyclone activity around the world, with the biggest increase over the Caribbean and the US East Coast. [P24] That's a model of the past, not a forecast. But it shows how far the Sahara reaches.

## ACT 7 — WHAT'S ACTUALLY HAPPENING
Meanwhile, something real has been happening on the Sahara's southern edge. Since the droughts of the 1970s and 80s, satellites have watched much of the Sahel grow greener. [S12] Returning rain explains much of it. Farmers explain some. [S13] In Niger, farmers protecting trees that sprout on their own land covered about 7 million hectares by 2017, around 17 million acres, according to a USGS estimate. [S14]

In 2007, African nations launched the Great Green Wall, aiming to restore 100 million hectares of land by 2030. [S17, S18] By 2020, the official count inside its core zones was about 4 million hectares. [S19] The project has shifted from a literal wall of trees to a patchwork of restored farmland, pasture and forest. [S21]

## CLOSE
So, could we turn the Sahara green? In patches, we already are. All of it would take water we'd have to make from the sea, energy on a scale nobody has built, and changes to weather far beyond Africa that we still can't fully predict.

And here's the twist. The Sahara will probably turn green again on its own. One scientist estimates the next orbital window is roughly 10,000 years away. [P23 — ATTRIBUTED]

So the real question isn't whether the desert can be green. It's whether we should be the ones to flip the switch.
