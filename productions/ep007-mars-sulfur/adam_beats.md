These yellow crystals are on Mars.

Nobody staged this picture. A rover drove over a rock, the rock cracked open, and this was inside.

It is pure sulfur, the first ever found on Mars.

And according to the mission's own project scientist, it shouldn't be there.

So how did it get there? Scientists now have a serious answer. But they don't all agree on it.

May thirtieth, twenty twenty-four. NASA's Curiosity rover, a car-sized robot, is climbing a valley on the side of a mountain on Mars.

The mountain is called Mount Sharp. It rises about three miles, five kilometers, from the floor of Gale crater.

Curiosity has been driving up its lower slopes since twenty fourteen, reading the layers like pages in a history book.

The team was not looking for sulfur that day. The rover was simply driving.

One of its aluminum wheels rolled over a pale rock, and the rock broke apart.

There is no video of that moment. What exists are the photos the rover took in the days after.

A few days later, the camera on the end of the robotic arm moved in close.

Yellow crystals, hidden under a reddish, dusty crust.

In this view from the mast camera, the patch of broken pieces is about five inches, thirteen centimeters, across.

But yellow doesn't prove anything. Plenty of minerals are yellow.

So the team used another instrument on the same arm: APXS, the Alpha Particle X-ray Spectrometer.

It holds a small radioactive source against the rock and reads the X-rays that come back. Every element answers with its own signature.

This material answered as sulfur. Not sulfur locked inside another mineral. Elemental sulfur, on its own.

It's the same yellow substance you can find around volcanic vents on Earth.

Now, sulfur on Mars is not news.

Curiosity had spent months in a region rich in sulfates: minerals where sulfur is bound to oxygen and to metals like calcium and magnesium. They are thought to have formed as water evaporated, long ago.

Pure sulfur is something else. It only forms under a narrow range of conditions.

On Earth, we usually find it near volcanoes and hot springs.

And around this part of Mount Sharp, there is no evidence of either.

Curiosity's project scientist, Ashwin Vasavada, put it plainly. It shouldn't be there. So now we have to explain it.

And it wasn't just one rock.

Look at the floor of the channel. Those bright stones scattered across it are the same kind of rock.

Close up, their outsides are white and full of holes. Inside, they are sulfur.

Here is the strange part. From orbit, this patch looked unremarkable.

The HiRISE camera on the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter can spot the rover itself, this dark dot, from space. But it could not resolve the stones.

To see them, you had to drive into them.

The channel is called Gediz Vallis. It winds down the side of Mount Sharp, and it formed late, when Mars was already drying out.

Floods of water and debris, flows of mud and boulders, and dry avalanches all left their marks here.

Water also bleached pale halos into some of the rocks. One scientist on the team summed it up: this was not a quiet period on Mars.

The sulfur stones were too small and too brittle to drill.

So engineers picked a large rock nearby, nicknamed Mammoth Lakes, and found a place where the rover could park on loose, sloping ground.

On June eighteenth, Curiosity drilled the forty-first sample hole of its mission. In this panorama, its arm is still raised after drilling.

The rock powder went into small laboratories inside the rover.

Curiosity spent months in this channel. On October eleventh, twenty twenty-four, it took one last look at the sulfur field and drove on.

The question stayed behind.

In August twenty twenty-six, the team published its analysis in the journal Science.

Their main result: these stones are native sulfur, and they appear to have formed right where they sit. They were not washed in from somewhere else.

The team estimates they formed roughly three billion years ago.

Their proposed explanation starts deep underground. Magma released sulfur-rich gases.

The gases rose and cooled in frozen ground near the surface, and, likely with help from water, turned into pure sulfur.

Much later, as the valley eroded, the deposit was exposed.

That is a proposal, not a closed case. Another group has suggested something quite different.

In their idea, a meteorite impact upstream melted a sulfur-rich layer of rock, and liquid sulfur flowed about four kilometers down the valley before it froze.

But that idea depends on a rock layer that nobody has measured yet.

One thing this discovery is not: evidence of life.

The team says it sees no sign that microbes played any role.

What the stones do show, the authors argue, is that ancient Mars had a way to concentrate sulfur, driven by fluids rising from below.

And they seem to have sat almost untouched for billions of years. One of the researchers called Mars a frozen snapshot.

So here is where it stands.

We know what the stones are, where they are, and roughly how old they are.

We don't know for sure how they got there. Part of the answer may sit in rock layers upstream that Curiosity has not reached yet.

Not bad, for a rock the rover only found because it ran it over.
