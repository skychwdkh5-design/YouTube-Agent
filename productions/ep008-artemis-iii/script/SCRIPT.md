## s03 · 0:21 · Why it matters + promise
*Facts: F01, F02, F06, F13*

NASA made this change back in February, and in October it published the full plan: fourteen days in orbit, two Moon landers, and one test that has to work before anyone lands near the lunar South Pole. Here's what they'll actually do up there, and why the landing now waits for Artemis Four.

## s04 · 0:43 · What NASA changed in February
*Facts: F03, F04, F01, F02, F24*

Artemis Two's flight took ten days: launch on April first, a seven-hour lunar flyby on April sixth, splashdown off California on April tenth. But by then the plan for the next mission had already changed. On February twenty-seventh, NASA rewrote the order of the Artemis program. Artemis Three had been the landing. It became a test flight in low Earth orbit, set for twenty twenty-seven, and the first landing moved to Artemis Four, targeted for twenty twenty-eight. NASA also promised at least one landing every year after that. NASA's reasoning, in its own announcement: keep the rocket and spacecraft as close as possible to the version that already flew, fly more often, and build up step by step, with each mission big enough to make progress but not so big that it takes unnecessary risk. NASA's administrator said that's how the agency did it in nineteen sixty-nine.

## s05 · 1:42 · The hardware already exists
*Facts: F15, F16, F18, F17, F14, F22*

And this isn't a paper mission. The four main engines for Artemis Three reached Kennedy Space Center in July. The Orion capsule and its service module were joined in late July. On September thirtieth, technicians put the nose cones on the twin boosters, finishing the stack. More than two hundred and fifty parts from Artemis Two's Orion are being reused on later missions, and NASA says reused parts move some installation work up by about three months. Even the launch tower is being turned around for its next flight nearly fifty percent faster. And that missing upper stage? It isn't scrapped. NASA reassigned it to Artemis Four, the mission that actually has to reach the Moon.

## s06 · 2:29 · Who flies
*Facts: F05, F23*

The crew is Randy Bresnik, Andre Douglas and Frank Rubio of NASA, and Luca Parmitano of the European Space Agency. In September they trained with Navy helicopter crews off San Diego, practicing being hoisted out of the water after splashdown. They've drilled escaping a capsule underwater, trained in their orange survival suits, and tasted nearly two hundred items of space food. Their mission will be about two weeks long, and it will never get farther than a few hundred miles from home.

## s07 · 3:02 · Three launches
*Facts: F11, F09, F25*

Artemis Three needs three launches. First, Blue Origin launches a test version of its Blue Moon lander. It can wait in orbit for up to thirty days. Then the crew launches on NASA's SLS rocket from Florida. Starship goes up later, after the crew is already in orbit, because it's built for a much shorter stay in space than Blue Origin's lander.

## s08 · 3:27 · The orbits
*Facts: F06, F27*

The orbits are precise. The Blue Moon test article circles about two hundred seventy-three miles up. Orion starts in a long, stretched orbit, from eighteen to more than eleven hundred miles, then rounds it out near three hundred forty-five. Starship is planned lower still, at two hundred six nautical miles, an orbit NASA picked for communications coverage. Every number here comes from NASA's flight profile.

## s09 · 3:54 · Dock one: Blue Moon
*Facts: F07, F08, F09, F28*

First docking: Orion pulls up alongside the Blue Moon lander. While they're joined, Orion's own software flies both spacecraft. Then the hatch opens. Up to two astronauts, in their orange survival suits, float inside. NASA calls it its first evaluation of a commercial lunar lander with a crew. They spend about two days checking its cabin, controls and life support. Riding along is a dummy the weight of a spacesuit, wired with sensors, reporting what the cabin really feels like.

## s10 · 4:26 · Dock two: Starship
*Facts: F10, F19*

Second docking: Starship. The test vehicle is a hundred seventy-one feet tall, about fifty-two meters, with a docking port in its nose. This time Orion uses its forward docking port and meets Starship nose to nose, a different geometry from the first docking, and SpaceX flies the pair. It lasts up to two hours, and nobody goes inside. NASA and SpaceX are still deciding exactly which control and communication tests to run.

## s11 · 4:55 · Home
*Facts: F29, F12, F06*

After Starship, NASA has set aside up to five more days for extra tests: more checks of the crew's survival suits, docking sensor tests, sound and heat measurements, and studies of how the crew lives and works on board. Then, after about fourteen days in total, Orion comes home and splashes down in the Pacific, off the coast of California, the same ocean where Artemis Two landed in April.

## s12 · 5:23 · Why docking first
*Facts: F30, F02, F13, F08, F10, F03*

So why spend a whole mission on this? NASA's Artemis program manager put it plainly: every piece of the mission in Earth orbit is designed to reduce risk before astronauts go back to the Moon. Three spacecraft on three different rockets, working together, which he says NASA has never attempted. And on Artemis Four, the landing depends on exactly this step. Orion can't land. A commercial lander has to meet it, take astronauts down near the South Pole, and bring them back. Distance is part of the logic. Artemis Two needed several days just to get back from the Moon. From low Earth orbit, a spacecraft can be back on the ground in a matter of hours, though the exact time depends on where it is and when it can land. So the first test of this handoff happens close to home.

## s13 · 6:20 · Apollo did this too
*Facts: F21, F24*

If this sounds familiar, it should. In March nineteen sixty-nine, Apollo Nine tested the lunar module in Earth orbit. Four months later, Apollo Eleven landed.

## s14 · 6:31 · What is still unknown
*Facts: F02, F18, F19*

What we don't know yet: a launch date. NASA says twenty twenty-seven, and nothing more precise. The rocket's fueling test at the pad is still ahead. And NASA hasn't said which lander will carry the first crew down on Artemis Four.

## s15 · 6:48 · Close
*Facts: F02, F13*

So the next Moon mission stays close to home, on purpose. If both dockings work, the following crew goes all the way down. Artemis Four is the landing. Artemis Three is the test it can't skip.
