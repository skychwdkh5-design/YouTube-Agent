## s03 · 0:21 · Why it matters + promise
*Facts: F01, F02, F06, F13*

NASA made this change back in February, and in October it published the full plan: fourteen days in orbit, two Moon landers, and one test that has to work before anyone lands near the lunar South Pole. Here's what they'll actually do up there, and why NASA decided the Moon can wait a year.

## s04 · 0:43 · What NASA changed in February
*Facts: F03, F04, F01, F02, S1*

Artemis Two's flight took ten days: launch on April first, a seven-hour lunar flyby on April sixth, splashdown off California on April tenth. But by then the plan for the next mission had already changed. On February twenty-seventh, NASA rewrote the order of the Artemis program. Artemis Three had been the landing. It became a test flight in low Earth orbit, set for twenty twenty-seven, and the first landing moved to Artemis Four, targeted for twenty twenty-eight. NASA also promised at least one landing every year after that. At the announcement, NASA's leadership argued that rare flights are a problem in themselves: crews and launch teams lose practice between missions, and changing the rocket and spacecraft after every flight adds risk instead of removing it.

## s05 · 1:34 · The hardware already exists
*Facts: F15, F16, F18, F17, F14, F22*

And this isn't a paper mission. The four main engines for Artemis Three reached Kennedy Space Center in July. The Orion capsule and its service module were joined in late July. On September thirtieth, technicians put the nose cones on the twin boosters, finishing the stack. More than two hundred and fifty parts from Artemis Two's Orion are being reused on later missions, and NASA says reused parts move some installation work up by about three months. Even the launch tower is being turned around for its next flight nearly fifty percent faster. And that missing upper stage? It isn't scrapped. NASA reassigned it to Artemis Four, the mission that actually has to reach the Moon.

## s06 · 2:20 · Who flies
*Facts: F05, F23*

The crew is Randy Bresnik, Andre Douglas and Frank Rubio of NASA, and Luca Parmitano of the European Space Agency. In September they trained with Navy helicopter crews off San Diego, practicing being hoisted out of the water after splashdown. They've drilled escaping a capsule underwater, trained in their orange survival suits, and tasted nearly two hundred items of space food. Their mission will be about two weeks long, and it will never get farther than a few hundred miles from home.

## s07 · 2:54 · Three launches
*Facts: F11, F09*

Artemis Three needs three launches. First, Blue Origin launches a test version of its Blue Moon lander. It can wait in orbit for up to thirty days. Then the crew launches on NASA's SLS rocket from Florida. Starship goes up after the crew is already in orbit.

## s08 · 3:13 · The orbits
*Facts: F06*

The orbits are precise. The Blue Moon test article circles about two hundred seventy-three miles up. Orion starts in a long, stretched orbit, from eighteen to more than eleven hundred miles, then rounds it out near three hundred forty-five. Starship flies lower still. Every number here comes from NASA's flight profile.

## s09 · 3:34 · Dock one: Blue Moon
*Facts: F07, F08, F09*

First docking: Orion pulls up alongside the Blue Moon lander. While they're joined, Orion's own software flies both spacecraft. Then the hatch opens. Up to two astronauts, in their orange survival suits, float into a lunar lander for the first time since Apollo. They spend about two days checking its cabin, controls and life support. Riding along is a dummy the weight of a spacesuit, wired with sensors, reporting what the cabin really feels like.

## s10 · 4:04 · Dock two: Starship
*Facts: F10, F19*

Second docking: Starship. The test vehicle is a hundred seventy-one feet tall, about fifty-two meters, with a docking port in its nose. Orion meets it nose to nose, and this time SpaceX flies the pair. It lasts up to two hours, and nobody goes inside. NASA and SpaceX are still deciding exactly which control and communication tests to run.

## s11 · 4:28 · Home
*Facts: F12, F06*

After about fourteen days, Orion comes home and splashes down in the Pacific, off the coast of California, the same ocean where Artemis Two landed in April.

## s12 · 4:39 · Why docking first
*Facts: F02, F13, F08, F10*

So why test this in Earth orbit? Because on Artemis Four, the landing depends on exactly this step. Orion can't land. A commercial lander has to meet it, take astronauts down near the South Pole, and bring them back. If that handoff fails near the Moon, the crew is days from home. In low Earth orbit, they're hours away.

## s13 · 5:03 · Apollo did this too
*Facts: F21*

If this sounds familiar, it should. In March nineteen sixty-nine, Apollo Nine tested the lunar module in Earth orbit. Four months later, Apollo Eleven landed.

## s14 · 5:14 · What is still unknown
*Facts: S2, F18, F19*

What we don't know yet: a launch date. NASA says twenty twenty-seven; its administrator told reporters in October that next summer is still the target. The rocket's fueling test at the pad is still ahead. And NASA hasn't said which lander will carry the first crew down on Artemis Four.

## s15 · 5:34 · Close
*Facts: F02, F13*

So the next Moon mission stays close to home, on purpose. If both dockings work, the following crew goes all the way down. Artemis Four is the landing. Artemis Three is the test it can't skip.
