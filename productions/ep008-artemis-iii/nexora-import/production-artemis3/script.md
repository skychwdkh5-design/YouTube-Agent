## Part 1 - What changed (0:20-1:35)

[SCENE: p1-title]
Artemis III was supposed to be the landing.

[SCENE: p1-feb]
On February 27th, 2026, NASA announced a new order. [[feb-change]]
NASA plans Artemis III as a test flight in low Earth orbit, in 2027. [[feb-change]]
The first boots on the surface moved to Artemis IV, which NASA aims to fly in 2028. [[first-landing]]

[SCENE: p1-why]
The reason is in what the flight tests.
A landing needs a crew spacecraft and a lander to meet, connect and work together.
NASA plans to try exactly that on Artemis III - in orbit around Earth, before anyone tries it near the Moon. [[feb-landers]]
According to NASA, the goal is to reduce risk, validate hardware, and gain operational experience before a crewed landing. [[goals-risk]]

[SCENE: p1-quote]
NASA's Amit Kshatriya put the logic simply: keep testing like you fly. [[feb-why]]
Administrator Jared Isaacman framed it as standardizing the approach and raising the flight rate - safely. [[feb-why]]
And after the first landing, NASA plans at least one surface landing every year. [[feb-yearly]]

[SCENE: p1-hardware]
The hardware is real and already stacking up.
Four RS-25 engines for this rocket arrived at Kennedy in July. [[rs25]]
The same month, engineers joined Orion's crew module to its service module. [[orion-joined]]
And on September 30th, the twin boosters got their nose cones. [[noses]]

## Part 2 - Who flies (1:35-2:05)

[SCENE: p2-crew]
Four people will ride it: commander Randy Bresnik, pilot Luca Parmitano of the European Space Agency,
and mission specialists Andre Douglas and Frank Rubio. [[crew]]
NASA plans the mission to last fourteen days. [[days-14]]
In those two weeks, NASA expects them and the flight controllers to practice the rendezvous, docking, habitation and operational procedures a real landing needs. [[goals]]

[SCENE: p2-three]
And the flight has a detail no Apollo mission ever had:
three separate spacecraft, which NASA plans to launch on three different heavy-lift rockets, all meeting in Earth orbit. [[three-rockets]]

## Part 3 - The flight, step by step (2:05-4:50)

[SCENE: p3-step1]
Step one doesn't involve the crew at all.
NASA plans for Blue Origin to launch first - a test version of its Blue Moon Mark 2 lander - into a circular orbit around Earth. [[bm-first]]
It could wait up there for up to thirty days. [[bm-30]]

[SCENE: p3-step2]
Step two: only days later, NASA plans to launch Orion on its SLS rocket from Kennedy. [[orion-days]]
Orion is expected to start in a stretched orbit - eighteen miles at the low point, one thousand one hundred twenty-two at the high. [[orion-ellipse]]
Then it's expected to settle into a circle about three hundred forty-five miles up, to let its temperatures stabilize. [[orion-345]]

[SCENE: p3-step3]
Step three is patience. NASA plans two days for the crew to check out Orion's systems. [[checkout-2d]]

[SCENE: p3-step4]
Step four is the first big test: docking with Blue Moon.
Once connected, the crew is expected to open the hatch and go inside the lander. [[bm-dock-2d]]
NASA plans about two days of evaluations aboard. [[bm-dock-2d]]
Orion is expected to connect at a port beside Blue Moon's crew cabin. [[ports]]
February's announced plan listed tests of the docked vehicles, and integrated checks of life support, communications and propulsion. [[feb-tests]]
When Orion undocks, NASA plans to have completed its first evaluation of a commercial lunar lander with a crew. [[first-eval]]

[SCENE: p3-step5]
Step five: according to NASA, SpaceX launches a Starship Version 3 test article from Florida. [[ss-launch]]
It's expected to fly lower than Orion - at two hundred six nautical miles, an orbit NASA chose for communications coverage. [[ss-orbit]]
This time, NASA plans for Orion to use its forward docking system, lined up with the docking system at Starship's nose. [[ports]]
Orion docks with it in a test expected to last up to two hours. [[ss-2h]]
And this time, nobody is expected to go inside. The crew stays in Orion. [[ss-2h]]

## Part 4 - Why docking first (4:50-5:55)

[SCENE: p4-why]
Why spend a whole mission on this?
Because every Artemis landing depends on two vehicles from two different programs finding each other and locking together.
In Earth orbit, a problem means coming home in hours.
Near the Moon, it means days away from help.
If it works as planned, Artemis III moves that first meeting to the safest place NASA has. [[feb-change]] [[first-eval]]

[SCENE: p4-extra]
After Starship, NASA plans up to five more days in orbit for extra tests. [[extra-5d]]
The list NASA announced is practical: spacesuit evaluations, docking sensor tests, acoustic and thermal measurements, human-factors assessments, and science and technology demonstrations. [[extra-tests]]
Then Orion is expected to reenter and splash down in the Pacific, where NASA and the US Navy plan to recover the crew. [[splash]]

## Part 5 - What it means for the Moon (5:55-6:50)

[SCENE: p5-2028]
If the dockings work, the next step is the one everyone is waiting for:
Artemis IV, targeted for 2028, is what NASA plans as the first Artemis landing. [[first-landing]]
On October 23rd, NASA laid out how it wants to speed up that path - with Artemis III flying in Earth orbit first. [[oct23]]

[SCENE: p5-unknown]
What NASA hasn't announced is a launch date. The plan says 2027, and nothing more precise. [[feb-change]]
So here's the honest answer to the question on the screen:
Artemis III won't go to the Moon. It's the mission that has to work so the next one can.
