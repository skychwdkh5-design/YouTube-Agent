# EP004 - Jakarta Is Sinking: Satellites Reveal Why. DRAFT v2 (owner review; NOT approved). Every number maps to a used claim in story/claims.json via the claims= hint.
[VIS: claims=C01 label="ESA RADAR, 2017-2018, LINE OF SIGHT"]
In parts of Jakarta, the ground is sinking almost five inches in a single year. That is what a European Space Agency radar satellite measured in 2017 and 2018.
[VIS: claims=C45 derived="430" label="ORBIT - ILLUSTRATION"]
It was circling about 430 miles above the city. And it could feel the ground move by a few millimeters.
[VIS: label="THREE QUESTIONS"]
So here is what we are going to find out. Why is the ground falling? Why has one of the sinking areas slowed down? And why can't the same satellites see part of the coast?
[VIS: claims=C33,C37,C11 label="JAKARTA, JAVA, INDONESIA - LANDSAT"]
Jakarta sits on the northwest coast of the island of Java, in Indonesia. It is built on a river delta, crossed by thirteen natural and artificial rivers. Almost eleven million people live in the capital province. And by some estimates, as much as 40 percent of the city is already below sea level.
[VIS: claims=C45,C01 label="HOW RADAR MEASURES - ILLUSTRATION"]
Here is how a satellite feels the ground move. It sends a pulse of microwaves down and listens for the echo. Come back later, and compare. In 2017 and 2018, the two Sentinel-1 satellites could return to the same spot every six days. If the echo has shifted, the distance has changed, by millimeters.
[VIS: claims=C02 label="LINE OF SIGHT, NOT STRAIGHT DOWN"]
But there is a catch built into the method. The satellite measures the change in distance along its line of sight, at an angle, not straight down. So a map like this has to say what was measured, when, and in which direction.
[VIS: claims=C34,C03 label="THREE SUSPECTS"]
So why is Jakarta sinking? Scientists who have measured it for decades name three suspects. Groundwater pumped out from underneath the city. The weight of buildings and roads on soft, compressible ground. And the natural settling of young river mud. ESA's own caption points largely at the groundwater.
[VIS: claims=C37,C38 label="PIPED WATER, 2022 STUDY"]
Why so much pumping? One 2022 study found that piped water reached only 64 percent of the province. Its authors proposed extending the pipes first to the north and west, as one way to cut the over-pumping.
[VIS: claims=C32 label="SPRINGS BACK vs STAYS SQUEEZED - ILLUSTRATION"]
When groundwater is pumped out, soft layers of ground can compact. Researchers separate two things: ground that springs back a little if water returns, and ground that stays squeezed. This animation is an illustration, not a measurement.
[VIS: claims=C35 label="DECADES OF MEASUREMENTS"]
None of this is new. Surveyors, GPS crews, and radar satellites have been measuring Jakarta for decades. Between 1974 and 2010, their typical numbers were three to ten centimeters a year, depending on the place and the time. That is roughly one to four inches a year.
[VIS: claims=C05,C06 derived="30" label="HOTSPOTS 2016-2024, LINE OF SIGHT"]
A recent analysis of radar data from 2016 to 2024 found three hotspots: one in the west, one on the northwest coast, and one in the northeast, near the harbor at Tanjung Priok. The fastest spot sank about 30 millimeters a year, roughly an inch, along the line of sight.
[VIS: claims=C39 label="SIX BOWLS - PREPRINT, 2014-2025"]
But count the hotspots a different way, and you get a different answer. A separate study, still a preprint, combined radar and GPS from 2014 to 2025 and counted six main subsidence bowls. In the worst one, the ground dropped up to 7.7 centimeters a year, about three inches. Across the whole metro area, the slow background sinking was about 1.1 centimeters a year. Its authors point out that it relies on a single continuous GPS station.
[VIS: claims=C40 label="BY CITY, 2019-2020 - ONE STUDY"]
Look at it district by district. A radar study of 2019 to 2020 found the ground sinking by an average of 4.9 centimeters a year in North Jakarta, 4.8 in West Jakarta, 3.1 in Central Jakarta, 2.8 in the South, and 2.5 in the East. The fastest sinking was near the coast and the river mouths, where the ground is made of soft river deposits.
[VIS: claims=C12 label="ARTIFICIAL ISLANDS - ONE SCIENTIST'S ESTIMATE"]
Out on artificial islands, one scientist told reporters the ground could be dropping about 80 millimeters a year. That is more than three inches.
[VIS: claims=C41,C36 label="WHAT IT DOES ON THE GROUND"]
On the ground, you can see the damage. That same study reported damaged roads at Mutiara Beach, West Cengkareng, and Pademangan. Other researchers list cracked buildings and roads, changed river flow, bigger flooded areas, and drains that stop working.
[VIS: claims=C09,C05 label="WHAT RADAR CANNOT SEE"]
Now the catch. To trust a pixel, the radar echo has to stay consistent from one pass to the next. In the 2016 to 2024 analysis, any pixel with a coherence below 0.6 was thrown out. And that filter removes pixels along the northern coast, the part of Jakarta closest to the sea.
[VIS: claims=C10 label="CHECK AGAINST GPS"]
Scientists check the radar against GPS. At one station, the radar rate came out 1.64 millimeters a year higher than the GPS vertical rate, with a typical error of 9.40 millimeters. And that station is not in the same spot as the radar pixel, so even this check has limits.
[VIS: claims=C07,C08 label="THE TWIST"]
Now the twist. The western hotspot started sinking fast in 2016, and then slowed down significantly. The northwest and northeast hotspots did not. They kept sinking in a straight line.
[VIS: claims=C27,C29,C31 label="2026 STUDY - ABSTRACT ONLY"]
A 2026 study, which we have only read as an abstract, also reports that some areas are slowing, a few even show localized uplift, and the coast keeps sinking. Slowing down is not the same as coming back up.
[VIS: claims=C14,C44,C15 label="WHY IT MATTERS"]
Why does it matter? Jakarta already floods. A flood in 2007 covered more than 70 percent of the city. On New Year's Eve 2019, a rain gauge at Halim Perdanakusuma recorded 377 millimeters of rain in a single day, nearly fifteen inches. And global sea level was rising about 3.3 millimeters a year. In the worst spots, the ground drops far faster than that.
[VIS: claims=C42,C43 label="SEA WALLS vs SINKING GROUND - 2017 MODEL"]
Along the waterfront, seawater already tops the coastal dykes, according to a survey of residents. And a 2017 model found that a three-meter dyke, enough for today's conditions, could lose its ability to stop floods completely by the year 2040, if the ground keeps sinking quickly. Its conclusion: stopping the sinking would be the most effective countermeasure.
[VIS: claims=C02 label="CHECK THE LABEL"]
So what do satellites prove? That parts of Jakarta are sinking, at different speeds, and that some of that sinking has slowed. What can they not prove? What happens where the radar goes blind, and exactly what is driving each bowl. And the next time you see a map of a sinking city, check the label. What was measured, when, and in which direction.
